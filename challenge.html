Predict the next pulse.

Beamline publishes a signed random value every minute. It claims you cannot know that value before it is published. Say what the next one will be, and if you are right, the prize is yours.

Make your prediction

The target is the full 512-bit output the beacon will publish — 128 hex characters, exactly as it appears in every pulse. Your prediction is accepted only while its round is still in the future, and you get back a receipt signed by the same key that signs the pulses.

128 hex characters. Anything else is rejected before it is stored.

    


    

      “Try to cheat” is there to fail. It submits the
      round that has just been published, together with that round’s own output
      — a guaranteed-correct answer. The server refuses it anyway, because it
      arrived after the fact, and that refusal is the entire mechanism. A receipt that
      could be issued once the answer was known would prove nothing to the person
      holding it, which is you rather than us.
    

  






  

    
What you are actually up against

    

      

        
The output is 512 bits of SHA-512. There are about
        1.34 × 10154 values it could take,
        and the beacon draws from a quantum source, live space-weather readings, and the
        kernel CSPRNG, mixed and run through a NIST SP 800-90A DRBG.

        
For scale, the observable universe holds roughly 1080 atoms. One
        guess per atom would cover essentially none of the space.

        
So: nobody is going to win this by guessing. That is the design, not a
        loophole. A challenge you could win by luck would be a raffle, and it would tell
        you nothing about whether the beacon is sound.

      

      

        
Then why run it? Because the interesting attack was never
        guessing. It is finding a flaw — a seeding bug, a predictable timestamp
        component, a DRBG misuse, a source that turns out to be replayable. If one
        exists, the prediction becomes cheap, and this page is where you would prove it.

        
Losing attempts are not wasted. Every prediction is scored on
        how many leading bits it shared with the real output. Against an honest beacon
        that averages exactly 1.0 bit. The scoreboard below reports the running mean, so
        the failures accumulate into a public test for bias that anyone can recompute
        from the published pulses.

The part where we argue against ourselves

    
The operator of this beacon is also the person holding the prize.
    That is a conflict, and pretending otherwise would be the least trustworthy thing on
    this page.

    

      

        
What protects you. Your receipt is signed by the same Ed25519
        key that signs every pulse in the chain. Denying a winning receipt would mean
        repudiating the key the entire chain hangs from — which destroys the product
        far more thoroughly than paying out does. Resolution is string equality, run
        automatically the moment your round lands. Nobody adjudicates anything.

      

      

        
What does not. Beamline can emit a pulse, dislike it, and
        publish the next one instead. It is a documented gap in the threat model, and it
        applies here: an operator seeing a winning prediction could in principle withhold
        that pulse. It is not invisible — the cadence is fixed, and a withheld round
        leaves a hole that /v1/beacon/verify-chain reports to anyone who
        asks. Watch the chain, not the promise.

      

    

    
Four of the five breaks Beamline invites need no server at
    all — forging a chain, passing an uncommitted draw, making the Python and
    JavaScript verifiers disagree, or demonstrating statistical bias. See
    Try to break it.
    This page covers the one that needs a beacon that is actually running.
